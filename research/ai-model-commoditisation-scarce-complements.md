# AI model commoditisation and scarce-complement thesis

**Captured:** 2026-09-22  
**Trigger:** Steve Eisman CNBC interview, *'Big Short' investor Steve Eisman on AI: The companies are trying to manufacture a crisis*  
**Source supplied by user:** https://www.youtube.com/watch/4qV5WWgFTS8  
**Status:** Cross-theme hypothesis note  
**Confidence:** Medium on commoditisation direction; Low on claims about incumbent motive or ecosystem protection

## Why this matters to Unicorn

This interview is useful less as a generic "AI bubble" argument than as a challenge to identify **where durable economic rents remain if frontier intelligence becomes increasingly substitutable**.

That question connects directly to Unicorn's existing bottleneck approach: do not assume the most visible layer captures the value. Look for scarce complements whose economics can strengthen as an upstream capability becomes cheaper and more abundant.

## Eisman's argument

**FACT — attributed to Eisman:** In the supplied interview, Steve Eisman argues that open-weight models are taking share, frontier AI companies lack durable moats, and safety/crisis narratives could lead to regulation that creates incumbent protection. He also argues that the AI capital chain is unusually dependent on the future health of OpenAI and Anthropic.

**INTERPRETATION:** The economically useful part of this thesis can be expressed without accepting the motive claim:

> open/open-weight capability improves → frontier-model differentiation narrows → price/performance competition intensifies → model-layer rents face pressure → value may migrate toward scarce complementary assets.

**HYPOTHESIS:** Compliance-heavy regulation could increase fixed costs and therefore advantage well-capitalised incumbents even when the underlying safety concerns are genuine. This effect does not establish that incumbents intentionally manufactured a crisis.

## What we should retain

### 1. Model commoditisation is a bottleneck-migration signal

If capable models become cheaper and more interchangeable, the investable question becomes less "who has the smartest model?" and more:

> **What remains scarce when intelligence becomes abundant?**

Potential scarce complements include:

- proprietary/high-information data and feedback loops;
- workflow integration and distribution;
- compute, networking and memory;
- power, cooling and electrical infrastructure;
- safety/evaluation/deployment infrastructure;
- physical systems that let intelligence sense, manipulate and act in the real world.

This is consistent with the existing Physical AI thesis that general foundation-model weights may commoditise faster than deployment feedback, calibrated simulation/evaluation, safety, runtime integration and physical industrialisation.

### 2. Separate AI demand from frontier-lab concentration risk

**HYPOTHESIS:** A material failure or retrenchment at a frontier lab could expose overbuild or financing risk in parts of the AI infrastructure chain.

But the stronger research formulation is not "the whole chain collapses if OpenAI or Anthropic fails." Aggregate compute demand can come from multiple labs, hyperscalers, enterprises, sovereign systems, open-model deployments, scientific AI and physical AI.

Therefore infrastructure underwrites should test:

- customer concentration;
- contract quality and cancellation exposure;
- who funds committed capacity;
- reuse/fungibility of assets if a customer weakens;
- whether demand survives substitution between model providers.

### 3. Regulatory moat is an effect to monitor, not a motive to assume

**HYPOTHESIS:** Expensive evaluations, cybersecurity requirements, audits, reporting, licensing or liability obligations could raise barriers to entry.

**OPEN QUESTION:** How much of future AI regulation creates genuine safety externality control versus fixed compliance costs that reinforce scale advantages?

Do not infer intent from the economic effect.

### 4. Safety evidence and existential-risk narratives are different claims

The thesis should distinguish:

- speculative catastrophic/AGI scenarios; from
- observable cybersecurity, autonomy, reliability and accountability risks.

Evidence for the second does not establish the first. Equally, evidence of competitive pressure does not establish that safety concerns are fabricated.

## Implication for the Unicorn research framework

**INTERPRETATION:** Treat AI commoditisation as a **bottleneck migration** hypothesis.

A useful cross-theme map is:

| Layer | If model intelligence commoditises |
|---|---|
| Frontier model | Differentiation and pricing power may compress |
| Application/workflow | Can retain value through proprietary data, distribution, integration and switching costs |
| Compute/networking/memory | Exposed to aggregate AI workload rather than one model winner, but concentration/capex-cycle risk matters |
| Power/cooling/electrical | Physical capacity, lead times and grid constraints can remain scarce |
| Physical AI | Cheap intelligence increases the importance of deployment data, safety, integration, actuation, sensing and reliable physical execution |

This does **not** automatically promote any company or theme. It strengthens the screening question:

> **Does this company own a scarce complement whose value persists—or increases—as model capability diffuses?**

## Open questions

1. How quickly is price/performance convergence occurring across closed and open-weight models?
2. Which AI infrastructure suppliers have the greatest customer/concentration exposure to OpenAI and Anthropic?
3. Which physical-infrastructure assets are genuinely fungible across model customers?
4. Which listed companies possess scarce complements rather than merely AI-correlated revenue?
5. Does increasing agent autonomy shift more economic value toward safety, identity, permissions, observability and real-world execution?
